# CFPs, selection, and the first talk

Use this file when the audit runs with `--for speaking`, or when a talk appears in the action list.

## Read the actual call before drafting anything

Every rule below is general. Everything *specific* about a given event — its deadline, its tracks,
its formats and lengths, whether review is blind, its stated review criteria, whether it covers
travel, whether it accepts previously-given talks — is a fact about the outside world that changes
every year and cannot be known from this repository.

**Fetch the call page now, quote it, and date it.** Anything you write about an event carries
`(checked <URL> on YYYY-MM-DD)` or it does not ship. Never state a deadline from memory: a stale
deadline is worse than no deadline, because it is acted on. If the page cannot be fetched, write
`DEADLINE UNVERIFIED — fetch <URL> before drafting` and stop treating the event as targeted.

Record per event: name, URL, checked-on date, format lengths quoted, tracks quoted, review policy
quoted, stated criteria quoted, deadline quoted or `NOT STATED ON PAGE`.

## How selection works, as a hypothesis to check

**Everything in this section is a prior, not a fact about any committee**, and the event's own
stated criteria beat it every time. Where the call publishes its review criteria, quote them,
date them, and rank the proposal against those — then say plainly where they contradict the list
below, because the call wins and this file loses. Where the call publishes nothing, this is what
you have, and it is labelled as a working assumption in the deliverable rather than asserted.

Treated that way, the properties worth engineering into a proposal are:

- **A bounded, concrete topic.** "What we learned migrating a 400-table schema without downtime"
  survives; "The future of data engineering" does not, unless the person is being invited rather
  than reviewed.
- **Evidence the speaker did the thing.** The proposal itself should make it obvious that the talk
  comes from doing rather than from reading. Specifics do this; adjectives do not.
- **Something the audience can use afterwards.** A committee is assembling a programme for
  attendees; write for that job rather than for a ranking of speakers.
- **A clear takeaway, stated as a sentence**, not "attendees will learn about X".
- **Evidence the talk is already structured.** Assume you will not be asked to clarify anything
  after submission, and that a proposal reading as aspirational is read as a risk to the schedule.
- **Fit with the track and the stated criteria**, which is why the page is read before drafting.

None of this requires a following, and a proposal is not improved by implying one. Do not tell the
person that a specific war story "beats" a known name, or quote an acceptance rate, a proposal
count or a committee's composition: those are facts about a particular event in a particular year,
knowable only from that event's own published page and only on the date you read it.

## Anatomy of a proposal that gets picked

Six parts, in this order, each one paragraph or less:

1. **The problem in the audience's terms.** Who has it, and when it hurts.
2. **Why the obvious approach fails.** This is the sentence that proves you have done it. If you
   cannot write it, the talk is not ready, and no amount of editing the abstract fixes that.
3. **What you actually did.** Concrete, with the constraint that shaped the decision.
4. **The takeaway.** One sentence, in the imperative, that an attendee could apply on Monday.
5. **Who it is for and what it assumes.** Naming the wrong audience is a rejection reason a
   committee will not write to you, so name the right one yourself.
6. **What you personally did**, if the work was a team effort. One line. It costs nothing and it
   removes the reviewer's largest unspoken doubt.

**Blind review.** Where the call says review is blind — quoted from the page, with the date
checked — strip every identifying detail from the abstract body (employer, product name, "at my
company we") and put it only in the speaker field. Treat a leak as disqualifying whether or not
that event says it is: you cannot know how a given committee handles one, and the cost of
assuming the strict reading is a paraphrase.

**Never in a proposal:** a talk you have not thought through to the end; an outcome you did not
measure; a claim about the audience's problem you have not checked; a co-speaker who has not
agreed; a demo you cannot run offline.

## Building a first speaking record from nothing

This is the honest route, and it is slower than the dishonest one. Say so plainly rather than
implying a shortcut exists.

1. **An internal talk at your own workplace.** Zero gatekeeping, a real audience, real questions.
   Record it if permitted; if not, keep the slides.
2. **A local meetup.** Most people skip this step on the assumption that they would be turned
   away. Do not assume in either direction: find the group's own page or organiser, check whether
   they are currently calling for speakers and on what terms, and record the URL and the date
   checked. An unanswered assumption is what makes this step get skipped.
3. **A lightning talk, or a smaller track at a regional event.** Shorter, and the recording is the
   same artifact as a long one.
4. **A full session**, proposed with the recordings from steps 1–3 attached.

At every step: **record it, publish the recording and the slides on a domain you own, and link
them from the next proposal.** A ten-minute meetup recording is a real speaking record and goes in
the claim ledger as rank 3 evidence. "I would be a good speaker" is not a record, evidences
nothing, and is cut like any other unbacked claim.

Practice against the clock at least twice before submitting a proposal, not before the talk. A
talk that does not fit its slot is discovered by the committee only after acceptance, which is the
expensive moment.

## Claims about speaking that are cut on sight

The claim ledger's rules apply here without exception:

- A talk you did not give, or that was cancelled.
- A venue you were not accepted to. "Submitted to" is not "spoke at".
- A keynote that was a session, a session that was a lightning talk, an invited talk that was
  reviewed like any other.
- An audience size you did not count, or a room capacity presented as attendance.
- A co-presented talk claimed in the first person singular.
- An "award-winning" or "top-rated" claim without the published rating or the awarding body's own
  page, fetched and dated.

Each cut is recorded in the ledger with its date, like any other. The recording, the programme page
and the slides are the evidence; where none of the three exists, the talk is not on the profile.
